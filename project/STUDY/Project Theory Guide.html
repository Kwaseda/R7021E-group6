1The project in one page

You are carrying a long plank through a house. If you pretend the plank is a giant ball as wide as its longest diagonal, you won't fit through any door, even though you obviously can if you walk straight through with the plank pointing forward. If you pretend it's three small balls in a row, you fit through the door, and you still notice when swinging around would knock the wall. That is research question 1.

Now someone walks across the hallway. If you only look at where they are, you walk into the spot they are about to step into. If you guess where they will be, you wait or go behind them. That is research question 2.

Lab 2 gave us an MPC that drives a TurtleBot Burger to a goal and keeps it out of circles. Two things in it are frozen:

  1. The robot is a single disc of 0.105 m.
  2. Every obstacle stands still, fixed before the solver is built.

The project un-freezes both, one step at a time:

3Lab 2 MPC, recapped

The model: a unicycle

Three states, the pose: x, y, θ. Two inputs: forward speed v and turn rate ω.

ẋ = v cos θ     ẏ = v sin θ     θ̇ = ω The robot only moves where it points. There is no sideways velocity term: that is the non-holonomic constraint.
A shopping trolley with fixed wheels. You can push it forward and twist it, but you can't slide it sideways. To move sideways you have to turn, drive, and turn back.

The optimisation problem, solved 10 times a second

At every tick, from the current pose, find the best N future inputs:

minu₀…uN−1   Σk=0N−1 q ‖pk − rk‖²  +  qN ‖pN − rN‖²  +  Σk R ‖uk − uk−1‖²  +  ρ Σ ε pk = predicted (x, y) at step k · rk = reference at step k · ε = slack on soft constraints

subject to:

xk+1 = f(xk, uk)    x0 = measured pose (delay-compensated)
0 ≤ v ≤ 0.22 m/s    |ω| ≤ ωmax    keep-outk − ε ≤ 0,   ε ≥ 0
TermLab 2 valueIn plain words
q (lterm)1.0"be near the reference all along the way"
qN (mterm)1.0"and especially at the end of the plan"
R (rterm)0.01penalises changes in input, not size: smoothness

Receding horizon

Driving at night with headlights that reach 40 m. You plan the next 40 m, turn the wheel a little, and a moment later you plan the next 40 m again from where you are now. You never drive the whole old plan.

How do-mpc turns ẋ = f(x, u) into steps

We give do-mpc a continuous model. By default it discretises with orthogonal collocation (Radau, degree 2): inside each 0.1 s step it fits a small polynomial through the states and forces the polynomial's slope to match ẋ = f(x, u) at the collocation points. It is more accurate than Euler (x + dt·f) for the same step. One sentence is enough if you're asked; it is a library default we didn't change.

4Time-varying parameters, the one mechanism we keep reusing

A TVP is a number the solver treats as fixed while it solves, but that we can overwrite before every solve, separately for every horizon step. Rebuilding the solver takes seconds, and overwriting a TVP costs nothing.

A conveyor belt of sticky notes, one per future moment. Before every plan you rewrite the notes: "at step 3 the goal will be here, the obstacle will be there." The solver reads the notes and never asks where they came from.
Scenarios 3 and 4 add no new solver machinery. They only change what we write on the notes. In Lab 2 task 4 we already wrote a different goal into each horizon step, so the obstacle TVP is the same mechanism pointed at a different variable.

5From a dot to a plank

The MPC model is a point (the axle centre). Real bodies have size. The trick is to grow the obstacle by the body's size, or equivalently to describe the body as discs and require every disc to stay out of every obstacle.

One disc vs three discs

How big must each disc be?

Cut the plank (length L, width W) into n equal slices along its length. Each slice is a small rectangle L/n by W. The smallest disc covering a rectangle has radius equal to half its diagonal:

rn = √( (L / 2n)² + (W / 2)² ) n = 1: r = √(0.25² + 0.10²) = 0.269 m  ·  n = 3: r = √(0.0833² + 0.10²) = 0.130 m

Why three? Three is the smallest n that fits the proposal's 0.30 m gap, since two discs need 0.32 m. After that, returns fall fast: the fourth disc saves 24 mm and costs a third more constraints. The radius can never drop below W/2 = 0.10 m, however many discs you use.
Lab 2's single 0.105 m disc was centred on the axle, but the Burger's chassis box is centred 32 mm behind the axle. Its rear corners sit at (−0.102, ±0.07), which is 0.124 m from the axle. So Lab 2 under-inflated the real body by about 19 mm. It never mattered, because the soft margins were bigger, but it is a good example of why the reference point matters. In the project the plank is the widest thing, and it is centred on the axle by design (Q3).

6Discs that turn with the robot

The discs sit at fixed offsets di along the robot's own x-axis (body frame). To get their world position, rotate the offset by θ and add the robot position:

cx,i = x + di cos θ     cy,i = y + di sin θ     di ∈ { −0.167, 0, +0.167 } m This is the same rotation as Lab 1's body-to-world transform, with the sideways offset equal to zero.
Holding a broom by the middle. Walk forward and the broom stays in your lane. Spin around and the ends sweep a big circle. The three discs are three hands' worth of broom, and the MPC knows where each hand is for any heading.

How straight must the plank be inside a 0.30 m gap?

Seen from the side of the gap, a plank tilted by φ from the gap axis sticks out by

half-width(φ) = (L/2) sin φ + (W/2) cos φ = 0.25 sin φ + 0.10 cos φ

For the real rectangle to fit a 0.30 m gap, half-width must be ≤ 0.15 m, so φ ≤ about 12°. The three-disc model is a little stricter than that. So the gap forces the robot to line up with it. We don't add a heading term to the cost, because the discs already supply the heading information.

7The keep-out constraint

For disc i (radius rd) and obstacle j (centre oj, radius ro), the distance between their centres must be at least ro + rd. Squared on both sides:

gij = (ro,j + rd)² − [ (cx,i − ox,j)² + (cy,i − oy,j)² ] ≤ 0 ≤ 0 means "outside". One of these per disc, per obstacle, per horizon step.
Why squared?
No square root√ has an infinite slope at zero distance, and IPOPT needs smooth gradients everywhere.
CheaperSquares are multiplications.
Same feasible setBoth sides are positive, so d ≥ R ⇔ d² ≥ R².

Count for one solve: discs × obstacles × horizon steps. Scenario 2 with 3 discs, 2 posts and N = 40 gives 240 keep-out constraints, against 40 in Lab 2 task 2. That's why solve time is one of our four metrics.

Code shape. The Lab 2 loop over obstacles, plus an inner loop over discs:

for j, (ox, oy, r_obs) in enumerate(obstacles):
    for i, d in enumerate(disc_offsets):          # (0.0,) for one disc, (-0.167, 0, 0.167) for three
        cx = x + d * ca.cos(th)
        cy = y + d * ca.sin(th)
        keep = r_obs + disc_radius
        mpc.set_nl_cons('obs%d_disc%d' % (j, i),
                        keep ** 2 - ((cx - ox) ** 2 + (cy - oy) ** 2),
                        ub=0.0, soft_constraint=True, penalty_term_cons=penalty)

Switching footprint:=one makes the offsets (0.0,) and the radius 0.269. The same loop then produces the Lab 2 constraint shape, which is why one parameter gives us the whole comparison.

8Hard vs soft constraints

A hard constraint must hold. If no input can satisfy it, the problem is infeasible and the solver returns nothing useful. A soft constraint can be broken, at a price. do-mpc adds a slack ε ≥ 0:

g(x, u) − ε ≤ 0,   ε ≥ 0,   cost += ρ · ε do-mpc adds the slack linearly to the cost. ρ = penalty_term_cons = 10 000 in Lab 2.
Finding 7d. A constraint is enforced at every horizon step including step 0, and step 0 is the measurement, which the optimiser cannot change. If odometry or delay puts the measured pose 1 mm inside a hard keep-out, then every input sequence is infeasible, forever. Lab 2 task 4 deadlocked at 0.2248 m against a 0.225 m radius and never moved again.

Why "soft is identical to hard when hard is possible"

A linear penalty is an exact penalty. If ρ is larger than the constraint's Lagrange multiplier (roughly, how much the cost would improve per unit of violation), the optimum puts ε = 0 whenever that is feasible. Lab 2 measured this: soft and hard runs agreed to within 1 mm whenever the hard run was feasible. The slack only gets spent when the alternative is no answer at all.

A hard rule is "never step on the grass, and if you're already on it, freeze forever". A soft rule is "stepping on the grass costs €10 000 a step". You never pay unless you're already on it, and then you pay a little to walk off.
The slack is spent against the inflation margin, not against the real object. The disc radii are conservative (the corners of each slice are covered with room to spare), so a few millimetres of slack still leaves the real plank clear. That's exactly why we measure the real rectangle distance from ground truth (Q4): it tells us whether slack ever turned into contact.

9The soft boundary box

Lab 2 used mpc.bounds['lower','_x','x'] = −B, which is hard. Task 1 overshot to x = 1.061 against B = 1.0, and task 4 started outside its box. Both were then unrecoverable. The project replaces the bounds with four soft constraints on the axle point:

x − B′ ≤ 0    −B′ − x ≤ 0    y − B′ ≤ 0    −B′ − y ≤ 0     with B′ = B − 0.269 Shrunk by the plank's sweep radius, so the plank stays inside B at any heading. 4 constraints, not 12 (Q5).

The box also does double duty in scenario 2: its y-limits are the corridor walls that stop the robot going around the posts.

10Horizon, reach and solve time

reach = N · Δt · vmax N = 20 → 0.44 m  ·  N = 40 → 0.88 m  ·  N = 60 → 1.32 m (Δt = 0.1 s, vmax = 0.22 m/s)
Finding 7b/7c: an obstacle is only avoidable if the detour it forces is smaller than the reach. Lab 2 task 2's 1.105 m keep-out needed about 2.2 m of swing against 0.44 m of reach, so the robot drove to the edge and stopped. No cost weight fixes that.

The tension: a longer horizon sees further (scenario 3 needs to see the obstacle coming), but the number of constraints grows with N and with the number of discs, and the solve must finish inside one 0.1 s tick. Our rule (Q6): sweep N ∈ {20, 40, 60} at the desk with the scenario-2 geometry, take the largest N whose 95th-percentile solve time is under 50 ms, half the tick. The other half leaves room for ROS, the odometry callback and slow outliers.

The 0.50 m row is the headline. The real plank has 15 cm to spare on each side, and the one-disc controller still refuses the gap. That's the cost of a conservative footprint.

What "passes" means. The goal is reached and the true rectangle clearance stays above zero for the whole run. Soft constraints can squeeze a too-big disc model through a gap by paying slack, so "reached the goal" alone isn't enough. If the one-disc run squeezes through at 0.50 m, that's a finding too, about ρ, and we report it.

Symmetry (finding 7a). In Lab 2 an obstacle sitting exactly on the start-to-goal axis deadlocked the robot, because left and right detours cost the same. Here the gap is on the axis and the posts are off it, so the straight line is the unique best path and there's no tie to break.

The obstacle centre stops being a constant baked into the solver and becomes a TVP, written per horizon step from its known trajectory:

Crossing a road. Looking only at where the car is now says "the road is empty in front of me". Knowing its speed says "it'll be right here in two seconds, so wait".

Metric: the smallest real distance to the moving obstacle, future vs now-only. Details belong to 20–23 Oct, after scenarios 1 and 2.

14Scenario 4 preview: the estimated goal

The goal moves, but its future is not handed to us. We fit a constant velocity to the last few observed positions (least squares on a line) and extend it across the horizon:

v̂ = Σ (tm − t̄)(pm − p̄) / Σ (tm − t̄)²     ĝ(t + kΔt) = plast + v̂ · kΔt The slope of the best straight line through position vs time, done separately for x and y.

15Measuring the truth

The controller believes discs. The world contains a rectangle. If we graded the run with disc distances, we'd be asking the model whether the model was right. So the analysis uses Gazebo's ground-truth pose and the exact rectangle (Q4).

Distance from a rectangle to a circle

  1. Move the obstacle centre into the plank's frame: p = Rᵀ(θ) · (o − (x, y))
  2. Clamp it onto the rectangle: q = ( clamp(pₓ, −L/2, L/2), clamp(p_y, −W/2, W/2) )
  3. Distance between the surfaces: ‖p − q‖ − ro. If it's ≤ 0 there was contact.

17Questions you will be asked

Why three discs, not two or five?Three is the smallest number that fits a 0.30 m gap: two need 0.32 m, three need 0.26 m. Each extra disc adds one constraint per obstacle per step and saves less and less width. The radius can never go below W/2 = 0.10 m.
Why not model the rectangle exactly?Distance to a rectangle has corners and edges, so its gradient jumps, and IPOPT wants smooth functions. Discs give smooth quadratic constraints. We do use the exact rectangle in the evaluation, where smoothness doesn't matter.
What is the non-holonomic constraint and why does it matter here?The robot can't move sideways (ẏbody = 0). It's why the plank must be along the heading, and why lining up with a gap takes distance, which costs reach.
Why are constraints squared?No square root, so the gradient stays smooth near zero and the evaluation is cheap. Both sides are positive, so the feasible set is identical.
Soft constraints mean you might hit the obstacle. Why accept that?With a linear penalty and a large ρ, the slack is zero whenever a collision-free plan exists, which is the exact-penalty property, and Lab 2 confirmed it to 1 mm. Slack only appears when the measured state is already inside, and then the alternative is a permanently infeasible solver. And the disc radii are conservative, so slack eats margin before it reaches the real object. We check that with ground-truth rectangle distance.
What is a TVP and why not just rebuild the solver?A parameter overwritten before each solve, one value per horizon step. Rebuilding takes seconds and the control tick is 0.1 s.
How did you choose the horizon?We measured it. Reach N·Δt·vmax must exceed the largest detour, and the 95th-percentile solve time must stay under half a tick. We took the largest N in {20, 40, 60} meeting both.
Why is the boundary box soft and shrunk by 0.269?A hard bound on a measured state is unrecoverable once it is crossed, which is Lab 2 task 4. 0.269 m is the plank's sweep radius, so the plank stays inside at any heading.
Where does the robot pose come from, and is it trustworthy?The controller uses /odom, which comes from wheel encoders through the diff-drive plugin and drifts in reality. The evaluation uses Gazebo ground truth, which is why the project is simulation-only: a repeatable truth and repeatable obstacle timing.
What does closed loop mean for an MPC whose plans are open loop?Each plan is open loop, but only its first input is used, and the next plan starts from a fresh measurement. Feedback enters through x₀.
What happens if the gap is narrower than 0.26 m?The three-disc model rejects it, even though the real plank (0.20 m) would fit down to about 0.20 m if perfectly straight. That's the remaining conservatism. More discs would shrink it toward 0.20.
