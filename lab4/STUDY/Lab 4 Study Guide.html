1The big picture

In Lab 3 the package slam_toolbox gave you a map and a pose. You used it as a black box. In Lab 4 you build that box yourself. The box is called Grid-FastSLAM 2.0.

1.1 The story in one paragraph

The robot drives through a maze. It has two sources of information. The wheels tell it roughly how far it moved. The laser scanner tells it how far away the nearest walls are in every direction. The filter keeps many guesses of where the robot has been. Each guess is a particle, and each particle also owns its own map. A guess is good when the newest laser scan fits the map of that particle. Good guesses survive and bad guesses are replaced. The robot's pose and the map are then the ones from the best particle.

1.2 Two sensors, two kinds of trust

Odometry (wheels)Laser scanner (lidar)
What it givesHow far and how much the robot turned since the last readingTypically 360 distances, one for each degree around the robot
How it goes wrongSmall errors add up. After a minute you are confidently wrong. This is drift.It does not drift. Each scan is accurate on its own, within a few centimetres.
Good atShort distances, smooth motionTelling where you are compared with the walls you can see
Bad atLong distances, wheel slipIt only shows the shape of what is near. Two identical corridors look the same.

Almost the whole lab is about using the second sensor to repair the first one.

1.3 The algorithm in five sentences

  1. Move every guess by the odometry plus a little random noise. The guesses spread out.
  2. For each guess, slide the new scan around a little until it fits that guess's own map best.
  3. Around that best fit, score a small cloud of nearby poses and draw the new pose from the cloud.
  4. Weight the guess by how well the scan fit, then add the scan to that guess's own map.
  5. Replace weak guesses with copies of strong ones, but only when too few guesses still matter.

The rest of this guide explains why each sentence is true and where it lives in the code.

2SLAM as a probability problem

Before any code, you need the vocabulary. SLAM is written as a question about probabilities. Once you can read the question, every later formula in this guide is a piece of its answer.

2.1 What is unknown and what is known

Unknown

The path of the robot: a pose (x, y, θ) at every time step. Written x0:t.

The map m: for us, the occupancy of every grid cell.

Known

The controls u1:t: the odometry increments.

The measurements z1:t: the laser scans.

The goal is the posterior: what you believe about the path and the map after seeing all the data.

p( x0:t , m  |  z1:t , u1:t )read: "the probability of this path and this map, given all scans and all odometry so far"

2.2 Why it is hard

There is a second problem: size. Your map is 400 by 400 cells, which is 160 000 numbers. A particle filter works by covering the likely region of the state space with samples. Covering a space with 160 000 dimensions is hopeless. Part 8 shows the trick that avoids it.

2.3 The Bayes filter: predict, then correct

All filters in this course (Kalman, particle, FastSLAM) are versions of one recipe. You keep a belief about the state. Each time step you do two things.

bel(xt) = η · p(zt | xt) · ∫ p(xt | xt-1, ut) · bel(xt-1) dxt-1
  • Predict. The integral. Take the old belief and push it forward with the motion model p(xt | xt-1, ut). The belief gets wider. This is Part 5.
  • Correct. Multiply by the measurement model p(zt | xt). Poses that explain the scan get more weight. The belief gets narrower. This is Part 6.
  • Normalise. The letter η here is just the number that makes the belief add up to 1.
Trap: two etas
In this recipe η is a normaliser. In the lab, η means something more specific: the sum η = ∑ τj from step 8 of the algorithm. It is still a number that makes things add up to one, but it also becomes the particle's weight. Part 10 explains why. Keep the two meanings apart in your head.

The Kalman filter carries this recipe with one Gaussian. The particle filter carries it with many samples. Lab 4 uses samples because a maze has symmetric corridors, and one Gaussian cannot say "I am in this corridor or that one".

Every symbol in this guide is listed with its code name in Part 16.

3A small probability toolbox

Five small tools. The code uses all of them in nearly every function, so it pays to be comfortable with them now.

3.1 The Gaussian and its logarithm

N(x; μ, σ²) = 1 / (σ√(2π)) · exp( −½ ((x − μ)/σ)² )

Take the logarithm and the exponential disappears:

log N(x; μ, σ²) = −½ ((x − μ)/σ)² − log σ − ½ log(2π)a parabola in the error, plus constants

This is the exact shape of the last lines of motion_model_log_pdf. There are three independent Gaussians, so the code has −0.5·sum(resid/sig)², then −sum(log sig), then −1.5·log(2π). The 1.5 is three halves, one half for each Gaussian.

3.2 Independent things multiply, so their logs add

If two measurements are independent, the probability of both is the product. A laser scan has hundreds of beams, and the lab treats them as independent. The probability of the whole scan is then a product of hundreds of numbers. In log form this becomes a sum:

log p(z | x, m) = ∑b log p(zb | x, m)

A sum of hundreds of moderate numbers is easy to handle. A product of hundreds of probabilities is a very small number, and over many time steps it gets smaller still. This is the main reason the filter stores log weights.

3.3 "Proportional to" and normalising

Weights only matter relative to each other. If three particles have scores 2, 6 and 2, they have the same meaning as 0.2, 0.6 and 0.2. To normalise means to divide every score by the total so that they add up to 1. In log form you subtract the log of the total. Symbol: a ∝ b means "a equals b times a constant that does not depend on what we care about".

3.4 The log-sum-exp trick

Often you hold the logs of some numbers and need the log of their sum:

log ∑i eai = m + log ∑i eai − m ,   m = max ai

Subtracting the largest value first keeps the exponentials between 0 and 1, so nothing overflows or collapses to zero.

Worked example

Take the log-values a = (−1000, −1001, −1005). A direct computation gives e−1000 = 0 in a computer (it is too small), so the sum is 0 and its log is −∞. With the trick, m = −1000 and the inner sum is 1 + e−1 + e−5 = 1.3746. The answer is −1000 + log(1.3746) = -999.682. That is correct and finite.

In the repo this is utils.logsumexp. It is used in the proposal (log_eta) and in weight normalisation.

3.5 Weighted mean, weighted spread, and the mean of angles

Given points xj with normalised weights wj:

μ = ∑j wj xj ,    Σ = ∑j wj (xj − μ)(xj − μ)T

Σ is the covariance: how spread out the cloud is, and in which directions. Heading angles need care. The average of +179° and −179° must be 180°, not 0°. The fix is to average the sine and the cosine separately and recover the angle with atan2. The code does this in improved_proposal.

3.6 Drawing a random sample from a Gaussian

To get a random pose from N(μ, Σ) you start from independent standard normal numbers and stretch and rotate them with Σ. In the code, rng.multivariate_normal(mu, sigma) does this. NumPy uses an SVD for this by default, which also copes with a Σ that has a zero eigenvalue.

  • Why a product of beam probabilities becomes a sum of logs, and what independence has to do with it.

4Occupancy grid mapping, Task 1

Task 1 is the one task that needs none of your five modules. It builds a map when the robot's poses are known. Every other task sits on top of it, because each particle in the filter owns exactly this kind of map.

4.1 The grid

The world is cut into square cells. In your params.yaml the map is 20 m by 20 m with 5 cm cells, so it has 400 × 400 cells. The map is centred on the robot's start pose. A world point becomes a cell with floor((point − origin) / resolution), which is GridMap.world_to_grid. Each cell stores one 32-bit number, so a whole map takes 0.64 MB. Remember this number. Every particle has a map of this size.

4.2 Probability, odds and log-odds

Each cell has a probability p of being occupied. Three ways to write the same belief:

FormFormulaUnknown cellLikely wall
probabilityp0.50.9
oddsp / (1 − p)19
log-oddsl = log( p / (1 − p) )02.2

The inverse is p = 1 / (1 + e−l). The code stores l, because log-odds make the update an addition.

4.3 Why the update is just a sum

A cell is either occupied or free, and that does not change while the robot drives. Each beam gives a bit of evidence. Bayes' rule in odds form says: new odds = old odds × evidence ratio. Take logs of both sides and the product becomes a sum:

lt = lt-1 + lsensor − l0

l0 is the log-odds of the prior. The lab fixes the prior at p = 0.5, which gives l0 = 0. That has two consequences you can use in the oral:

  • A cell that was never touched is exactly 0.0. The code uses this: known = log_odds != 0.0 decides which cells are drawn in RViz.
  • The update is "add one number". No division, no tracking of history.

4.4 The inverse sensor model

A normal sensor model answers: "given the world, what will the sensor read?" Mapping needs the opposite: "given the reading, what do I learn about the cells?" That is why it is called the inverse sensor model.

For one beam with measured range z, look at a cell at distance s along the ray. With a hit window of total width r:

s < z − r/2  →  free (add lfree)
s ≤ z + r/2  →  occupied (add locc)
s > z + r/2  →  no information (add nothing)

In the config, r = hit_window_cells × map_resolution = 2 × 0.05 = 0.10 m. Half of it, 0.05 m, is hit_half_m.

4.6 Clamping

Without a limit, a cell seen as a wall 100 times has log-odds near 85. After that it would take more than 200 free passes to forget it. The map could not change its mind. The clamp keeps every cell between −log_odds_limit and +log_odds_limit. With the limit at 5, the largest probability is 0.9933 (about 99.3%).

Worked example

The current values are pocc = 0.7 (adds +0.847), pfree = 0.4 (adds -0.405), limit 5, occ_thresh 0.6 (log-odds 0.405).

  • A wall cell hit 3 times: l = 3 × 0.847 = 2.54, so p = 0.927.
  • A floor cell crossed 4 times: l = 4 × (-0.405) = -1.62, so p = 0.165.
  • 2 hits and then 1 free pass: l = 1.289, so p = 0.784.
  • A saturated wall (l = 5) needs 12 free passes to drop below the 0.6 threshold. A saturated free cell (l = −5) needs 7 hits to rise above it. That is how fast a wrong cell can recover.

5The motion model, Task 2

The motion model is where the filter writes down how much it trusts the odometry. It is needed in two forms: one that draws a random pose, and one that scores a given pose. The two forms must describe the same distribution.

5.1 What odometry is

Odometry counts wheel rotation and adds it up into a pose. Each small increment is good, to a few millimetres. The sum is not, because every increment carries a little error and the errors never cancel. The pose you get from odometry is therefore only a good guess of change between two nearby moments, not of absolute position.

5.2 Splitting a move into turn, drive, turn

Between two odometry poses the filter does not look at (Δx, Δy, Δθ) directly. It splits the move into three numbers:

trans = √(Δx² + Δy²)
rot1 = atan2(Δy, Δx) − θprev
rot2 = θcur − θprev − rot1

Why three numbers? Each has a plain physical source of error: how well the robot turned, and how far it really drove. Noise on each one is easy to describe.

This split is given to you as utils.odometry_increment. It has one special rule. If the robot barely moves (trans < TRANS_EPS, one micrometre), the direction of travel is meaningless, so rot1 is set to 0 and rot2 takes the whole turn. Without this rule, a parked robot would get a random rot1 from rounding noise.

5.3 The sampling form (step 3 of the algorithm)

To spread the particles, add independent Gaussian noise to each of the three numbers, then apply the noisy move to the particle's own pose:

rot1' = rot1 + N(0, σrot1²)   trans' = trans + N(0, σtrans²)   rot2' = rot2 + N(0, σrot2²)
θ* = θ + rot1'
x' = x + trans' · cos θ*
y' = y + trans' · sin θ*
θ' = θ* + rot2'

5.4 The density form (step 7 of the algorithm)

The improved proposal makes up candidate poses near the scan match. For each one it must ask: how plausible does the odometry find this pose? To answer, run the split backwards. Take the previous pose and the candidate pose, compute the (rot1, trans, rot2) that would get you there, and compare with the real u:

resid = ( rot1cand − urot1 ,   transcand − utrans ,   rot2cand − urot2 )
log p = −½ ∑i (residi / σi)² − ∑i log σi − 1.5 log(2π)

The angle residuals are wrapped to [−π, π), so an error of 0.1 rad stays 0.1 rad on both sides of ±π. A candidate that matches the odometry exactly gets the highest score. With the first value, all σ = 0.02, the score at an exact match is −3 log 0.02 − 1.5 log 2π = 8.98.

5.5 Why the two forms must agree

Step 7 multiplies the laser likelihood by this density. If the density described a different distribution from the one used for sampling, the proposal would weigh candidates by a rule that nothing else in the filter obeys. The code keeps them in step by using the same order in both: turn, drive, turn. The desk check in lab4/LOG.md confirms it two ways [desk]: the spread of 20 000 samples with sd_trans 0.1 comes out at 0.1, and the log density at the exact pose equals the formula and agrees with utils.odometry_increment.

5.6 Two noises with similar names

odometry_sigmas is what the filter believes about its odometry, per filter step. You tune it on the robot. odom_noise_sigma is noise the node adds in simulation, per /odom message. It is set to zero on the robot. They are opposite things. Part 12.4 has the table and the numbers.

Optional depth: two simplifications in this model

First, the three noise terms are independent, but the position error and the heading error of a real robot are linked. The sampler partly recovers that link, because the first turn tilts the drive direction, which is the banana. The density does not describe that tilt exactly.

Second, the density is a density over the three increment numbers, not over the pose (x, y, θ). A strict change of variables would add a factor that depends on trans. The standard textbook version of this model, which the lab follows, leaves that factor out. For candidates a few centimetres apart this is a small effect. You can mention it if asked why the model is only an approximation. It is my reading of the maths, not a statement from the lab text.

6The measurement model, Task 3

The motion model says where the robot could be. The laser says where it is. The measurement model turns a scan into a number: given this pose and this map, how probable is the scan?

6.1 From beams to map points

A scan is a list of ranges, one for each beam angle. The helper scan_to_endpoints turns it into 2-D points in the robot frame: the spot where each beam ended. To test a pose, you apply that pose to all points and get their positions in the map frame. utils.transform_points does this for K poses at once and returns an array of shape (K, B, 2): K candidates, B beams, two coordinates.

6.2 Two classical answers

  • Beam model. Cast every beam through the map from the candidate pose and compare the expected range with the measured one. Accurate, but slow: you ray-trace for every candidate.
  • Likelihood field. Look only at where each beam ends. Ask how far that endpoint is from the nearest wall on the map. If the pose is right, endpoints sit on walls. The lab asks for this one.

6.3 The distance transform

For each cell, GridMap.likelihood_field computes the distance to the nearest occupied cell (probability above occ_thresh = 0.6), clipped at max_dist = 0.10 m. This is done once for the whole map. After that, each beam is a table lookup. The function is called once per particle per step, with all K candidates in a single call.

6.4 The probability of one beam

If d is the distance from the endpoint to the nearest wall, one beam has the probability

p(zb | x, m) = zhit · exp( −d² / (2σhit²) ) + zrand / zmax

The first term says: most of the time, the endpoint is near a wall, with a small Gaussian error. The second term is a flat floor: sometimes the sensor returns something random, and the model must never say "impossible". zmax is range_max, which makes the floor a uniform density over the sensor range. The weights zhit = 0.8 and zrand = 0.2 add up to 1.

6.5 The whole scan

The beams are treated as independent given the pose. So the log probability of the scan is the sum of the logs of the beams. This is the full model of Task 3:

log p(z | x, m) = ∑b log( zhit · exp(−db² / 2σhit²) + zrand / zmax )
Worked example: a scan of three beams

The three endpoints in the figure have distances 0, 0.05 and 0.12 m. The last one is clipped to 0.10 m. With the lab's constants:

  • d = 0: p = 0.8 + 0.2/3.5 = 0.8571, log = -0.154
  • d = 0.05: p = 0.8·exp(−0.125) + 0.0571 = 0.7631, log = -0.270
  • d = 0.12 → 0.10: p = 0.8·exp(−0.5) + 0.0571 = 0.5424, log = -0.612

The scan scores -0.154 + (-0.270) + (-0.612) = -1.036. A pose that moved all three endpoints onto the wall would score 3 × (-0.154) = −0.463. Higher is better. The best a beam can do is -0.154. The worst is -0.612.

6.6 What the numbers mean

  • A beam that is completely wrong costs only 0.458 more than a perfect beam, because of the clip at max_dist. So even a hopeless pose cannot lose more than about 165 in total on a 360-beam scan.

6.7 Two limits to remember

  • Beams are not really independent. A hundred neighbouring beams may hit the same wall. Treating them as independent makes the model more confident than it should be. Poses that are slightly off lose a lot. The consequence is spread-out particle weights and a low N_eff (Part 7). The knob likelihood_stride lets you use only every n-th beam for weighting. This is my reasoning about the model, not a measured result.
  • Only hit beams score a pose. A no-return beam has its endpoint placed at range_max. That point says nothing about a wall, so step() removes those beams before scoring (hits = ranges < range_max − 1e-6).
Trap: likelihood is not correlation
The scan matcher scores a pose by the average occupancy probability at the endpoints. That number is between 0 and 1, but it is not a probability of the scan. It is fine for picking the best of many candidates. It would be wrong inside τ, because τ must be an honest likelihood: its size changes μ, Σ and η. The docstring at the top of measurement_model.py says exactly this.

7Particle filters and resampling, Task 4

A particle filter keeps its belief as a cloud of weighted guesses. This part covers the general idea, the weights, and the resampling step you write in resampling.py.

7.1 The belief as a swarm

A Kalman filter stores one bell curve. A particle filter stores N guesses of the state, each with a weight. Dense clusters of heavy guesses mean "probably here". The cloud can have several clusters, so it can say "in this corridor or that one". This is why the lab uses it in a symmetric maze.

7.2 Importance sampling

Say you want samples from a target distribution f, but you can only draw samples from another distribution g, the proposal. You can still use the samples from g if each one carries a correction:

w(x) = f(x) / g(x)

Where g produces too many samples, the weight is small. Where g produces too few, the weight is large. The weighted samples then stand in for samples of f.

In the course
Lecture 9, slides 86 to 88 (importance sampling).

7.3 The plain particle filter

  1. Predict. Draw each new particle from the motion model, starting at its old pose. This is the proposal g.
  2. Weight. Give each particle the weight w = p(zt | xt). The target is "poses that explain the scan", the proposal is "poses the odometry allows", and the ratio is the measurement likelihood.
  3. Resample. Draw a new set of N particles, picking each one with a probability equal to its weight.

This version is called FastSLAM 1.0 when each particle also carries a map (Part 8). Remember the pattern: the weight is target divided by proposal. In Part 10 the proposal changes, and so does the weight.

7.4 Weights go lopsided: effective sample size

After a few steps, a few particles explain the scans well and most explain them badly. The weights become uneven. The filter still holds N particles, but only a few of them matter. The effective sample size counts how many:

Neff = 1 / ∑k (ŵ[k])² ,   for normalised weights ŵ

The two ends are worth remembering, and the handout tells you to test them: equal weights give exactly N, and all the mass on one particle gives exactly 1. Your desk check shows N_eff = 4, 1 and 2 for uniform, one-hot and two-halves weights with four particles [desk].

7.5 Resampling

Resampling turns weight into count. A particle with a large weight appears several times in the new set. A particle with a tiny weight disappears. The new set has equal weights, 1/N each.

It has a cost. The copies are identical, so the set loses variety. A guess that was removed cannot come back. If you resample too often, the whole set collapses onto one path long before the evidence supports that. So there are two questions: how to resample, and when.

7.6 How: systematic resampling

The simplest method draws N independent random numbers. That is noisy: a good particle might by bad luck get no copies. Systematic (also called low-variance or stochastic universal) resampling draws one random number u between 0 and 1. Then it places N sample positions evenly, 1/N apart:

posi = ( u + i ) / N ,   i = 0 … N−1

Lay the weights end to end on a line from 0 to 1. Each position lands in one particle's stretch, and that particle is picked. A particle with weight w is picked either ⌊Nw⌋ or ⌈Nw⌉ times, never fewer and never many more.

Worked example: the two boundary details in your code

cumulative[-1] = 1.0. The running sum of weights should end at 1, but rounding can leave it at 0.9999999. Then a position near the end would fall off the array. The code forces the last value to 1.

side='right'. Take weights (0, 0.5, 0.5) and u = 0. The positions are 0, 1/3, 2/3 and the running sums are (0, 0.5, 1.0). The first position, 0, lands exactly on the edge of particle 0, whose weight is zero. With side='right' it goes to the next particle, and the result is (1, 1, 2). With side='left' the result would be (0, 1, 2), which picks a particle with weight zero [desk].

8Rao-Blackwellization and FastSLAM 1.0

A particle filter cannot search 160 000 map cells with random guesses. Rao-Blackwellization is the trick that makes SLAM possible with a particle filter. It is the reason every particle owns a map.

8.1 The dimensionality problem

A particle filter works when the likely region of the state space can be covered by samples. The state of SLAM is the whole path plus the whole map. That is far too large. Most of that size is the map.

8.2 The factorisation

The posterior of Part 2 can be split in two with the chain rule:

p( x0:t, m | z, u )  =  p( x0:t | z, u )  ·  p( m | x0:t, z ) path posterior  ×  map posterior given the path

Look at the second factor. If the path is known, finding the map is mapping with known poses. That is exactly Task 1, and it is easy: add log-odds along each beam. Nothing needs guessing.

Key idea
Use particles only for the part that is hard, the path. For each path guess, compute the map exactly with the Task 1 update. That is why every particle owns its own occupancy grid. It also explains the name: Rao-Blackwellization means replacing sampling with an exact calculation wherever you can.

The cells of a grid are treated as independent of each other, given the path. That is what lets the map posterior split into a simple update for each cell.

9Scan matching

Step 5 of the algorithm is provided for you, but you need to understand it. Its output decides where the improved proposal looks, and its score decides whether the proposal is used at all.

9.1 The idea

Take the newest scan and slide it a little in x, y and heading over the particle's own map. Keep the placement where the beam endpoints land on cells that are most likely occupied. Think of sliding tracing paper over a floor plan until the lines match.

9.2 The score

For a candidate pose, transform the endpoints into the map, read the occupancy probability of the cell under each one, and average:

score = (1/n) ∑b p( cell under endpoint b )

Endpoints outside the map are left out of the average. The score is always between 0 and 1. A high score means the endpoints sit on likely walls. This is scan_matcher.correlation.

9.3 Coarse to fine

Checking every pose in a big window at fine spacing is expensive. So the matcher searches in two stages. Stage 1 checks a coarse 5 × 5 × 5 lattice over x, y and heading with a wide window. Stage 2 checks a finer 5 × 5 × 5 lattice around the stage 1 winner, with half the window. Both stages are derived from your two window parameters:

9.4 The pull toward the odometry

Among all candidates the matcher picks the one with the best score − 0.1 × penalty, where the penalty is a Gaussian-shaped cost for moving away from the odometry sample x̄:

penalty = ½ (dxy / σtrans)² + ½ (dθ / σrot)²

The two σ values come from odometry_sigmas (σtrans is the middle value and σrot combines the two rotation values). The weight 0.1 is the constant PRIOR_WEIGHT. The comment in the file calls this a heuristic on purpose: the score is not a log-density, so there is no exact exchange rate. The prior only changes which candidate wins. It is not part of the reported score.

Worked example: how far can the match really move?

This example uses the first value, σtrans = 0.02 m. The tuned 0.04 m makes the prior four times weaker. With σtrans = 0.02 m, moving the match 10 cm away from x̄ costs 0.1 × ½ × (0.10/0.02)² = 1.25 in the selection score. The score itself only lives between 0 and 1, so no gain from the scan can pay for that. The cost reaches 1 at about d = 0.02 × √20 = 0.089 m. If a realistic gain is 0.5, the limit is about 0.063 m.

So with these values the matcher can correct something like 6 to 9 cm per step, even though its window is ±20 cm. This is my arithmetic from the code, not a measured result. It shows that odometry_sigmas also sets how far the scan matcher can reach. A smaller σ means a shorter reach.

9.5 What the score is on an empty map

The handout asks one pointed question about match_score_min: "what does an unexplored map score?" Every unobserved cell has probability 0.5. So an empty map gives a score of exactly 0.5 for every candidate, and a map with a few scans gives a bit more.

Because the matcher only needs the location of the maximum, an unnormalised score is enough. The result is a single pose. The improved proposal then builds a real distribution around it, using the honest likelihood of Part 6.

9.7 What the matcher cannot do

  • It can only fix drift inside its reach (window and prior), per step.
  • It cannot tell positions apart along a long straight corridor: sliding along the wall changes nothing in the score.
  • It cannot see differences smaller than a cell.

10The improved proposal, Task 5

This is the core of the lab. The scan matcher gives you one pose. One pose is not a distribution, and the filter needs a distribution to draw from and a weight to give. Steps 6 to 10 of the algorithm build both. Your code is proposal.py.

10.1 The idea in one picture

Why not just use x*? If every particle moved to its own x*, every particle would sit on the same spot and the filter would stop representing uncertainty. The proposal keeps the answer close to x* and still gives it a width, so the particles spread a little around the best fit and can later be told apart by their weights.

10.2 τ: two models multiplied

τj = p( zt | xj, mt-1 ) · p( xj | xt-1, ut )algorithm step 7. Left factor: Task 3 (the laser likes this pose). Right factor: Task 2 (the odometry allows this pose).

A candidate gets a large τ only if both agree. A pose the laser loves but the odometry finds impossible gets a small τ. So does a pose the odometry expects but the scan rejects. That is why you wrote both models as density functions and not only as samplers.

10.3 Why the weight changes: the idea Lecture 9 spends most time on

In Part 7 the rule was: weight = target divided by proposal. In FastSLAM 1.0 the proposal was the motion model alone. The target contained the scan, so the ratio left over was the scan likelihood p(z | x̄, m). That is the weight.

Now the proposal is different. It already contains the scan. Two things follow.

  1. The old weight would be wrong. The scan has already shaped where the particle lands. If you also used the likelihood at that pose as the weight, you would use the same scan twice. Lecture 9 slide 204 is the warning: you cannot put the scan-match result into the filter directly.
  2. The new weight is a constant. Call the shape of the new proposal τ(x), divided by its total so that it adds up to one:
g(x) = τ(x) / η ,   η = ∫ τ(x') dx'

The target is τ(x) (the real posterior has this shape, up to a constant). So:

w = target / proposal = τ(x) / ( τ(x) / η ) = ηthe x cancels. Every sample from this proposal gets the same factor.
Key idea
The weight factor is η, the total mass of τ. It does not depend on which pose you sampled. It is a score for the particle: given this particle's map and its previous pose, how well could the odometry and the laser explain this scan, adding up every nearby pose that could have been the true one? A particle whose neighbourhood explains the scan badly gets a small η and loses weight. That is how a bad map or a wrong path is punished.

In the code the integral becomes a sum over the K lattice points: η = ∑ τj. A lattice sum equals the integral only up to a constant volume factor. Every particle uses the same K and the same window, so the factor is the same for all of them and disappears when the weights are normalised.

10.6 The Gaussian fit and its floor

From the weights wj the code computes the mean μ and the covariance Σ, the formulas of Part 3.5. Σ is a 3 × 3 matrix. Its diagonal holds the variances of x, y and heading. The off-diagonal entries say how the axes move together. If τ stretches along a diagonal line, those entries are not zero. The sampler rng.multivariate_normal(mu, sigma) then draws a pose that follows that shape.

Σ gets three small repairs:

  • The variance floor SIGMA_REG = (10−4, 10−4, 10−5). As a standard deviation that is 1 cm, 1 cm and 0.18°. If all the τ mass sits on one candidate, the scatter is exactly zero, and every particle would sample exactly μ. They would show no spread at all. The floor prevents this. It is also an honest limit: cells are 5 cm and the likelihood field is flat inside the wall band, so nothing in the scan can say the pose is known better than about a centimetre.
  • The eigenvalue floor SIGMA_EIG_MIN = 10−6. A covariance matrix must not have a negative eigenvalue, and rounding can leave one a tiny bit below zero. eigh splits the symmetric matrix into directions and sizes. The code lifts any size below the floor and builds the matrix again.
  • Exact symmetry, by averaging Σ with its transpose.

11.3 Why the map is updated last

This is point (a) of the handout. Each likelihood in the step must see mt-1, the map before the current scan. Think of a student who grades his own exam with the answer key he has just written from his own answers. He gets full marks whatever he wrote. A scan scored against a map that already contains that scan fits almost perfectly, at whatever pose it was added. Every particle would be rewarded for its own guess, and the filter would look better than it is. Nothing crashes. Plots look fine. That is why the handout calls this mistake "invisible".

In the code, integrate_scan is the last thing inside the loop (line 194). The matcher, the proposal and the fallback all run before it.

11.4 Weights through one step

  1. Each particle's log_weight grows by log η (or by the plain log-likelihood).
  2. _normalize subtracts the log-sum-exp of all log weights, so the real weights add up to 1. Then it gives weights and N_eff.
  3. best_index is read from the normalised weights now, because after a resample every weight is equal.
  4. _maybe_resample decides.
Worked example: should it resample?

Four particles with weights 0.1, 0.5, 0.3, 0.1 (the example of Part 7). Then N_eff = 1 ÷ (0.01 + 0.25 + 0.09 + 0.01) = 1 ÷ 0.36 = 2.78. With resample_threshold 0.5 the limit is 0.5 × 4 = 2. Since 2.78 ≥ 2, the filter keeps the particles as they are. With resample_every_step: true it would resample anyway. Task 7 deliverable 3 compares exactly these two policies. The N_eff written to the log is 2.78, the value before the decision.

11.5 Which particle is "the estimate"?

The node publishes the pose, the path, the map and the TF correction from best_particle() (grid_slam_node.py lines 336 to 341). That function returns the particle with the largest log_weight at the time of the call. Two facts follow, and they are good oral material:

  • On a step without resampling, it is the heaviest particle.
  • On a step with resampling, every new particle has log weight −log N. All weights are equal, and argmax returns the first. That is the first survivor in index order, not the heaviest of the old set. step() does record the heaviest in best_index, but the node uses that field only for the log.

The template gives best_particle() this way and we left it alone. A desk comparison of the two choices showed no difference in the pose error [desk]. The covariance in /best_particle is different: it is the spread of the whole weighted cloud.

13.3 The sweep

Code you read (given): tools/run_sweep.sh

The command from the handout is tools/run_sweep.sh runs/maze_drive "1 5 10 20 50" "1 2 3". For each proposal (fs2, then fs1), each N and each seed, the script launches the node without RViz, waits 5 s, plays the bag with --clock, then stops the node with SIGINT so that it saves its log. The logs are named fs1_n<N>_s<seed> and fs2_n….

Two proposals × 5 values of N × 3 seeds = 30 replays. Each one lasts as long as the bag plus the start-up, about 1.9 hours in all if they run one after the other (my estimate: 30 × (218 s + 5 s), not measured). Start it early.

Trap: the runs must see the same scans, and the right odometry for each scan
Real-time replay with scan_queue_depth 1 drops every scan that arrives while a step is still running. A run with large N has a slow step, so it processes fewer scans than a run with N = 1. Then the ATE values are not comparable. The Code session's rule: before comparing ATE, check that all runs have the same number of rows in the log.
A deep queue fixes the number of scans but creates a second problem. The node keeps only 100 odometry samples (line 62), which is about 2.3 s at 44 Hz [gazebo]. If the filter falls further behind than that, _odom_at can no longer find the odometry from the scan's own time and returns the oldest sample it has. The pairing is then wrong. Slowing the bag down (ros2 bag play --rate) avoids both. The script does not do that yet. This is reasoning from the code, not something I ran, and it is an open step (C9) in lab4/LOG.md.

13.4 ATE: the error metric

The Absolute Trajectory Error compares the estimated path with the true path.

  1. At each logged step, take the distance between the estimated position and the true position (x and y only; heading is ignored).
  2. Square them, average over all steps, and take the square root. That is the RMSE of this one run. evaluation.absolute_trajectory_error returns it, together with the mean and the maximum.

There is no alignment. Both paths start at the origin. Aligning them would hide a bad start. That is also why an early drift in heading counts in the number for the rest of the run.

Worked example

Four steps with position errors 0.02, 0.05, 0.11 and 0.18 m. Mean = 0.090 m. RMSE = √((0.0004 + 0.0025 + 0.0121 + 0.0324) ÷ 4) = 0.109 m. Maximum = 0.18 m. The RMSE is larger than the mean because squaring gives the big errors more say. One bad stretch moves the RMSE more than the mean.

In figure 2, one point is one run's RMSE. For each proposal and each N, the line is the mean over the seeds, and the shaded band runs from the smallest to the largest of them. The x-axis is logarithmic. A wide band means the result depends on the random draw, so a difference between two lines that is smaller than the bands says little.

13.5 N_eff against time

For figure 3 you replay the same bag twice at the same N: once with resample_every_step: true and once with false. The figure plots N_eff ÷ N against time, draws your threshold, and marks the steps at which the filter resampled. It drops the first step, where the weights are still equal by construction. The number of resampling events per policy is the count of true values in resampled.

This is why the log stores N_eff before the resample. With the other choice the every-step policy would show a flat line at N, and the depletion that made the decision would be invisible.
